## D34
**What it is** A teletext-style bento: every section is a numbered "page" (P100 Sommaire, 200 Vera, 300 Ideas ... 991-999 for settings), laid out as a dense grid of dark tiles with coloured top rules and a green CRT for Vera.

**What works**
- The page-number system is a real idea carried all the way through: the tab bar (Home 100, Vera 200, Ideas 300 ... Wishes 600), each page's badge (P300, P500, P990), the Home tiles, and the settings list numbered 991-999. Numbers give a strong sense of place.
- Each section keeps its own accent (gold Vera, lilac Ideas, blue Plans, teal To do) on the tile's top rule and its count, so the Home grid reads by colour as well as by name.
- The Ideas page grid of cards with a pixel glyph in the bottom-right corner (bike, fork and knife, tree, suitcase, car) is lively and easy to scan, and each card carries kind, drive time and who it is for in a regular order.
- The Plans month has a clear today cell (the orange-boxed 3) and readable event chips with time underneath.
- Focus states are bold and consistent: a bright green 2px frame on "Skip to the page", the Family link, "200 Vera" and "We should try...".

**What doesn't**
- Two navigation systems on every desktop page: the top bar (Memory, Family, Status, Settings) and the numbered tab strip beneath it. That is a lot of chrome before any content.
- The Home grid is cramped and uneven. Titles are truncated ("Mount St. Helens da...", "Nutcracker at the Ke..."), while the Restaurants tile is mostly empty space with a large "2" and a pixel fork, and the Memory tile is one line of text over a void.
- The Status page's spending monitor clips its own text: the last line reads "ILY LIMIT", cut off at the left edge of the screen.
- Small type everywhere: tile labels like "WAYS TO START", "LATELY ADDED", "SOMETHING TO EAT" and the meta lines under ideas are tiny grey caps, hard to read at the cards' scale.
- "P100 · SOMMAIRE" in French is an inside joke the family will not get.
- The chat page leaves a wide dark left column holding only a title, a paragraph and two kid names, while the conversation itself sits in a box with lots of empty right side.

**What's beautiful** Home, the Next up monitor: the phosphor "Oaks Park roller rink" in underlined green mono beside a radar with 1w/2w/4w rings, scanlines and a tiny power light, then "What's on your mind?" below a dashed rule. It is the best single object in the design.

**What's ugly** Status, the Spending monitor: the readout is cropped mid-word ("ILY LIMIT") and the progress bar of dots and brackets is crammed against the frame.

**What I'd do**
1. Pick one navigation: keep the numbered strip and fold Memory, Family, Status and Settings into it, or keep the bar and drop the strip.
2. Give the Home grid fewer, wider tiles so titles stop truncating, and drop or merge the near-empty Restaurants and Memory tiles.
3. Fix the clipped spending readout and give the monitor inner padding.
4. Raise the small caps labels a size and lift their contrast.
5. Replace "Sommaire" with plain English ("Contents").

**Verdict** 6.5/10. A memorable teletext idea and a lovely Home monitor, let down by crowding, double navigation and a clipped readout.

## D35
**What it is** A mission-control instrument panel: each page has a left column with its own CRT instrument (a dot plot of the month's plans, an analog spending gauge, a numbered radar of places, a to-do tally) over an "On this page" index, beside clean ruled tables with dotted leaders.

**What works**
- Each page gets an instrument that actually shows its data: Status has a needle gauge from 0 to the $2.00 limit with an average marker; Ideas has a radar whose numbered blips (#7, #4, ...) match the numbers in the list; To do has a monitor tallying Late 3, This week 0, Later 0, No date 1.
- The lists are tidy tables with leader lines running to right-aligned values ("27 min ↘ S", "Maya, Theo", "#1"), which is clean and quiet next to the bright monitors.
- The Status "On this page" index with small dot meters (●●●○ 3 of 4 on, 1 of 4 set) is a smart way to summarise a long page.
- Overdue items carry an amber dash and amber text ("was due 6 days ago") so lateness stands out without shouting.
- The phone Home is the most usable of this batch: a large writing box, a big full-width green Send, and the ways to start as buttons under it.

**What doesn't**
- The Home "Next up" chart is cryptic: dots on columns labelled 4, 9, 17, 24 against an hour axis of 09-21 with a "now" mark; a family member will not read it as "four plans this month" without study.
- The Ideas filter panel is a wall of chips (nine kinds, five people, four statuses) squeezed into the narrow left column under the radar.
- The left column repeats itself: "Ready · OpenAI · gpt-6-luna · $2.00 a day" sits at the bottom of every page's sidebar, and on Settings the sidebar's list of sections duplicates the main list beside it.
- Vera's nav item uses an empty circle as its icon, which reads as a radio button rather than a person.
- The green rail with connectors down the left of the To do list and the ruled "Coming up" timeline add lines that do not mean much.
- Home's second row (Finish setting up, then a narrow Coming up beside an "On this page" panel) feels like leftover space.

**What's beautiful** Status, the spending gauge: a curved phosphor dial with tick marks, a needle resting at zero, "$0.00 TODAY", "0% OF LIMIT", "AVERAGE DAY $0.04" and a boxed "LIMIT 2.00" marker. It is exact, legible and charming.

**What's ugly** Ideas, the filter chip block in the left column: three rows of kind chips, a row of people and a row of statuses, all the same grey, crowded under the radar.

**What I'd do**
1. Replace the Home dot plot with something readable at a glance, such as the next three plans as lines, or label the dots with the plan names.
2. Move the Ideas filters into a single compact bar above the table, or a collapsible panel, and let the radar have the column to itself.
3. Drop the repeated "Ready" footer from every sidebar except Status, and remove the Settings sidebar list that duplicates the page.
4. Give Vera a proper icon in the nav.
5. Remove decorative rails that carry no information.

**Verdict** 7.5/10. The best data instruments of the batch and clean tables, held back by a cryptic Home chart and a cluttered filter column.

## D36
**What it is** An editorial dashboard: tall condensed grotesque headlines paired with big coloured counts (Status 6, Ideas 12, Things to do 4), calm card grids, and the green CRT used once per page as a framed monitor.

**What works**
- The headline system is confident and useful: "Ideas 12 · in all · last added Sun 27 Sep", "Things to do 4 · open · 3 late", "October 2026 4 · ahead in view". Each title answers a question before you read the page.
- Home opens with a single strong sentence, "Give Vera something to plan." with "plan" in green, and the writing box directly under it; the monitor beside it shows Next up with a radar. Clear hierarchy, one job.
- The "Finish setting up" strip as a stepper (1 2 3, "Next: choose your own password →") is compact and tells you the next action.
- The Home below-the-fold summary strip (Wishes 0 open, Memory, Family 4 people, Status 1 of 3 lit, Settings 9 parts) is an elegant index of the rest of the app.
- The Ideas table is readable with columns for kind, idea, drive and who; the To do table shows overdue dates in amber and a "NOW" marker line between late and upcoming.
- Status's Answers/Keys/Links meters with small dot rows are tidy.

**What doesn't**
- The active state is a slate-blue pill (Home, Vera, Ideas tabs; "All ideas", "All 12", "Open 4", "Month") that sits awkwardly against the green and dark palette; it looks like a default control rather than part of the design.
- The chat page is a narrow column on the left with the right third of the screen empty.
- Ideas has two rows of filters (All ideas/Restaurants/Filter/Save a thought, then nine kind chips) before the table, which is a lot of controls for twelve items.
- Some phrasing in the strips is obscure: "Status 1 of 3 lit", "RANGE 4 WK / CONTACTS 4" on the monitor.
- The Plans month is plain; the cells are large and mostly empty, and event chips are small grey slabs.

**What's beautiful** Home, the hero: "Give Vera something to plan." in tall condensed type with the green "plan.", the writing box beneath, and the bezelled monitor with the radar and "Oaks Park roller rink" to the right. It is the most inviting first screen in this batch.

**What's ugly** The slate-blue active pills, most visible on Ideas ("All ideas" and "All 12" with a green dot in the corner): a cold, washed-out colour foreign to the rest of the palette.

**What I'd do**
1. Make the active state green-tinted (a green border or underline on dark) instead of slate blue.
2. Centre or widen the chat column, or put the kids' conversations beside it on desktop.
3. Collapse the Ideas kind chips behind the Filter control, leaving one row.
4. Rewrite the cryptic labels ("1 of 3 lit", "Range 4 wk") in plain words.
5. Give plan chips on the month their section colour so the calendar has some life.

**Verdict** 8/10. A well-ordered, confident editorial design with a great opening sentence, marred mainly by an off-palette active colour and an underused chat page.

## D37
**What it is** Retro pixel-computer: a chunky pixel display face for headings, window title bars with hatching and a little close box on every panel, a beige-bezelled CRT, and a vertical timeline rail down the left with a "NOW" marker and pink nodes.

**What works**
- The strongest personality of the four. The hatched title bars ("Who answers", "Coming up", "To do") make every panel look like a classic desktop window, and it holds across every page.
- The beige monitor bezel on Home and Ideas is a warm, unexpected touch against all the dark green.
- Active nav items are colour coded by section (green Vera, lilac Ideas, blue Plans, amber To do, green Status and Settings), so you always know where you are.
- The Settings page is a tidy 3 by 3 grid of windows, each with a boxed icon, a status line and an arrow; "Needs a look" in amber stands out.
- The Coming up list with tear-off calendar dates (OCT 9 Fri, OCT 17 Sat) is friendly and quick to read.

**What doesn't**
- The pixel face is used for too much: names, item titles and settings captions. At small sizes the dotted zeros make times hard to read ("Saturday 10:00 to 14:00" in the chat, "Up to $2.00 a day" on Settings).
- The left timeline rail with "NOW" and pink nodes runs down every page, including Settings and Status, where it marks nothing in time. It costs margin and adds noise.
- The To do page is very long: each task is a full window with a title bar, owner, an "Open" pill, a due date and "Edit, complete, or snooze", so four tasks fill the screen.
- Home below the fold mixes styles: Coming up and To do are windows, but Lately added is loose icon tiles floating without a frame.
- The Ideas radar monitor is a wide letterbox that pushes the actual list of ideas below the fold ("> 12 ideas" barely appears).
- The select control crop shows the select closed, not open, and the 14th Tab focus crop shows only a small checkbox, so the controls card says little.

**What's beautiful** Home, the Next up monitor in its beige plastic bezel: green "Oaks Park roller rink", "[TOMORROW]" boxed, a radar on the right and a slot and power light on the bezel below. It looks like a real object.

**What's ugly** The left timeline rail on Status and Settings: a dashed vertical line with pink square nodes hooked to each panel, and a "NOW" tag, on pages that have nothing to do with time.

**What I'd do**
1. Keep the pixel face for page titles and window title bars only, and set item names, times and money in the readable sans.
2. Keep the timeline rail only where it means something (Home, Plans, To do) and drop it elsewhere.
3. Compress To do into one row per task with the edit actions behind a single control.
4. Put Lately added on Home in a window like its neighbours.
5. Shorten the Ideas radar monitor or move it beside the list so the ideas show above the fold.

**Verdict** 7/10. The most characterful design of the four with a lovely beige monitor, but its pixel type and decorative rail cost readability and space.

## With the concept

### D34
**Does it change my reading?** Partly. Teletext page numbers belong to the same family of old screens, so the numbering now reads as a deliberate part of the concept rather than a quirk. But "easy to use" makes the faults weigh more, not less.
**What now reads differently** I called "P100 · Sommaire" an inside joke. I now see it as a teletext homage that fits the brief, though the word is still lost on an English-speaking family. The double navigation and the cramped, truncating Home grid count for more now, because they work against "easy to use". Outside the monitors, the phosphor character is thin: the tiles are a generic dark dashboard.
**Revised mark** unchanged (6.5/10)
It has the old-screen character in its numbering and its Home monitor, but it gives up too much ease of use to get it.

### D35
**Does it change my reading?** Yes. Its split now looks like the brief carried out on purpose: the phosphor character lives in one instrument per page, and everything you have to read or act on is modern, plain tables and big buttons.
**What now reads differently** The CRT instruments in every sidebar (gauge, numbered radar, to-do tally) read less as decoration and more as how the concept should work: green screens that show real data. The cryptic Home dot plot is still a fault, since it puts character ahead of being easy to read.
**Revised mark** 8/10 (up from 7.5)
Of the four, it does the best job of keeping the phosphor character in working instruments while leaving the rest modern and usable.

### D36
**Does it change my reading?** Slightly. It fully meets the "modern and easy to use" half, but the phosphor half is lighter than I had weighed: one framed monitor per page, the green cursor after FamilyDB and some glow. Beyond that, the condensed editorial headlines owe nothing to an old screen.
**What now reads differently** I faulted the slate-blue active state as off-palette. Against a phosphor concept it is worse, because it is the one colour with no link to a green screen. The big headline counts still work, but they read as magazine typography rather than terminal character.
**Revised mark** unchanged (8/10)
It is still the clearest and most inviting design, but it keeps its phosphor character in a few places rather than carrying it throughout.

### D37
**Does it change my reading?** Yes. Much of its personality comes from a different old computer: the pixel display face and the hatched window title bars recall early black-and-white desktops and 8-bit games, not a green phosphor terminal. Only the beige-bezelled monitor fits the brief squarely.
**What now reads differently** I praised it as the most characterful design. It is, but much of that character is off-concept, and the pixel face that supplies it is the very thing that hurts readability, which the brief's "easy to use" rules out. The beige monitor I called beautiful still fits.
**Revised mark** 6.5/10 (down from 7)
It has plenty of retro character, but much of it comes from the wrong kind of old computer and costs the ease of use the concept asks for.
